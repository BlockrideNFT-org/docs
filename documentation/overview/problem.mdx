## Problem Overview
Africa is home to more than one billion people, yet in 2019 fewer than 900,000 new vehicles were sold across the entire continent. In the same year, the United States sold more than 17 million.

This disparity is not a function of demand. It is a function of financing.

Limited access to vehicle financing has constrained fleet expansion, delayed modernization, and suppressed vehicle ownership across rapidly growing urban centers.

Most buses in operation today are used imports, often operating with worn-out engines, degraded interiors, and inefficient fuel systems. High-capacity buses remain financially out of reach for many operators. As a result, fleets rely heavily on smaller vehicles with lower passenger capacity.

Operators are not deploying aging assets by preference, but by necessity.

## From the Fleet Operator’s Perspective
Vehicle financing is characterized by high interest rates, substantial down payment requirements, and documentation-heavy approval processes. Capital access is slow, expensive, and administratively burdensome.

In rapid transit, where buses require materially higher upfront investment than personal vehicles, these constraints are magnified. Operators must commit significant working capital before assets begin generating revenue, limiting their ability to scale or modernize in response to demand.

The result is structural underinvestment in fleet quality and capacity.

## From the Investor’s Perspective

Vehicle financing has traditionally been limited to institutional investors or high-net-worth individuals. Participation presents three structural challenges:

1. **Lack of Trust:** Investors depend on operators or financing companies to report revenue accurately. In informal models, vehicle owners hand buses to drivers who may underreport earnings or mismanage assets. Even institutional investors rely on internal company reporting, exposing them to opacity and delayed visibility.

2. **High Barrier to Entry:** Financing buses—particularly new, high-capacity mass transit vehicles—is capital intensive. A single investor cannot easily fund acquisitions at scale. Participation remains restricted to those with substantial balance sheets, limiting capital inflow into the sector.

3. **Capital Efficiency:** Invested capital is typically locked for the duration of long-term financing contracts. Liquidity is limited, and reallocation is difficult. Returns are tied to extended repayment cycles rather than incremental, transparent revenue flows.

## The Structural Gap

[Africa’s urban population is projected to double by 2050](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1224205/forecast-of-the-total-population-of-africa/#:~:text=According%20to%20the%20forecast%2C%20Africa's,the%20Asian%20population%20by%202100.). Cities are expanding rapidly, commuter volumes are rising, and mass transit demand is accelerating.

Yet the financial infrastructure required to support this growth does not exist at scale. Operators lack affordable, flexible capital. Investors lack transparent, accessible entry points.

The gap between urban mobility demand and financing capacity is widening.

Without structural innovation in how transport assets are financed, fleet quality will stagnate while urban congestion intensifies.

The financing gap is not merely a constraint. It is the opportunity.
