## Problem Overview
Access to vehicle financing across Africa remains severely constrained. The continent has the lowest per capita vehicle ownership globally. In 2019, fewer than 900,000 new vehicles were sold across Africa, compared to over 17 million in the United States. This financing gap limits fleet expansion, delays modernization, and reduces the quality of mass transit.

Most buses in operation are used, often with worn-out engines, torn seats, and inefficient fuel systems. Seating is frequently modified to maximize revenue, compromising comfort and safety. High-capacity buses remain out of reach, forcing operators to rely on minibuses with limited passenger capacity. This increases motorization rates, urban congestion, and carbon emissions, creating a domino effect that magnifies the original financing problem.

## From the Fleet Operator’s Perspective
For fleet operators, vehicle financing is often characterized by high interest rates, substantial down payment requirements, and documentation-heavy approval processes. Capital access is slow, expensive, and administratively burdensome.

In rapid transit—where buses require materially higher upfront investment than personal automobiles—these constraints are magnified. Operators must deploy significant working capital before vehicles begin generating revenue, limiting their ability to modernize fleets or expand in response to commuter demand.

As a result, many operators continue deploying aging vehicles, not by preference, but by necessity.

## From the Investor’s Perspective

Vehicle financing has traditionally been limited to institutional investors or high-net-worth individuals. Participation comes with three structural challenges:

1. **Lack of Trust:** Investors must rely on operators or financing companies to report revenue accurately. In informal setups, individual vehicle owners hand vehicles to drivers, who may underreport earnings, mismanage vehicles, or default. Even institutional investors depend on company reporting, leaving them exposed to inaccurate or delayed information.

2. **High Barrier to Entry:** Financing vehicles—especially mass transit buses—is capital intensive. A single investor cannot easily fund multiple buses, restricting participation to those with substantial capital. This reduces the investor base and slows mass transit expansion.

3. **Capital Efficiency:** Invested capital is often locked for the duration of financing contracts. Investors cannot access funds in emergencies or reallocate capital easily, reducing liquidity and limiting portfolio flexibility. Returns are tied to long-duration repayment schedules rather than immediate or incremental revenue streams.

## The Structural Gap

The current financing system under-serves both operators and investors. Operators cannot access scalable, affordable capital. Investors face exclusivity, opacity, and inflexible investment structures. Fleet modernization is delayed, congestion intensifies, and environmental impacts worsen.

A new approach is required—one that improves accessibility, transparency, capital efficiency, and scalability while directly linking investor returns to vehicle performance.
